# Observation

`T2 Situation Investigation and Elicitation › 2.7 Observation and Immersion`

**Also described as:** Field Observation; Observation & Apprenticeship; Observation and Ethnographic Studies

This card merges 7 descriptions of the method, labelled Variant A–G in every field.

## Problem Solved

**[Variant A]**

Gathers firsthand insights into actual business practices and uncovers tacit knowledge, reducing reliance on filtered or inaccurate verbal accounts.

**[Variant B]**

Reduces requirements inaccuracies and missing details caused by unstated worker habits or inaccurate self-reporting by directly viewing work activities in context.

**[Variant C]**

Reduces tacit knowledge gaps, unstated routine habits, and discrepancies between official documentation and actual operational practice.

**[Variant D]**

Reduces the problem of tacit knowledge, unarticulated routines, and social/cultural workplace nuances that stakeholders cannot verbally explain.

**[Variant E]**

Uncovers tacit ('know-how') knowledge, unwritten real-world workarounds, and environmental constraints that users cannot easily explain in interviews.

**[Variant F]**

Elicits subconscious knowledge and dissatisfiers that users take for granted or cannot express verbally.

**[Variant G]**

Discrepancy between documented and actual processes

## Uncertainty Reduced

**[Variant G]**

Tacit knowledge; facts distorted in verbal accounts

## Step-by-Step Procedure

**[Variant A]**

Prepare objectives and checklist (or STROBE sheet).
2. Brief the worker and management on the purpose.
3. Observe the worker performing tasks over an extended period.
4. Record normal flows, exceptions, interruptions, and environment.
5. Debrief worker to clarify observed actions.
6. Document findings.

**[Variant B]**

Define clear observation objectives (e.g., process flow understanding, pain point identification).
2. Select approach: Active/Noticeable (ask questions during work) or Passive/Unnoticeable (silent observation).
3. Prepare participant: explain purpose, reassure performance is not being judged, get consent.
4. Conduct session: observe tasks, record steps, execution time, tools used, and workarounds.
5. Follow up with participant to ask clarifying questions and fill knowledge gaps.
6. Synthesize notes and validate findings with participants and management.

**[Variant C]**

Select target operational tasks and establish trust with subjects.
2. Perform passive observation (watching/recording without interference, protocol analysis, or long-term ethnography) OR active observation (participating in tasks).
3. Record activities, gestures, informal workarounds, and social interactions.
4. Analyze logs to infer implicit mental models and unstated constraints.
5. Organize debriefing meetings with observed personnel to validate findings.

**[Variant D]**

Gain access to the operational site and build trust with workers.
2. Observe tasks in passive mode (watching/recording) or active mode (participating/asking questions).
3. In protocol analysis, ask the subject to think out loud while performing the task.
4. In ethnographic studies, observe long-term social interactions, gestures, shortcuts, and environmental reactions.
5. Analyze field notes/recordings to extract implicit mental models and unstated operational needs.
6. Validate findings in debriefing sessions with workers.

**[Variant E]**

Choose observation style: Silent Observation, Talk-Through, or Apprenticeship (coached active participation).
2. Shadow the practitioner in their actual working environment.
3. Observe actual work sequences, tool usage, interruptions, and environmental factors.
4. In apprenticeship mode, perform simple tasks under practitioner coaching ('learning by doing').
5. Record field notes, sketches, or video logs (with permission).
6. Debrief with the practitioner immediately after the session to clarify observed actions.
7. Document the observed workflow and validate with the user.

**[Variant F]**

Obtain access to the operational workspace.
2. Observe users performing their routine work tasks.
3. Record process steps, pain points, workarounds, and user errors (optionally using audio/video).
4. Question observed processes to distinguish 'as-is' habits from true requirements.
5. Formulate target requirements that optimize inefficient steps.

**[Variant G]**

1. Agree the visit.
2. Passive or active observation of the work.
3. Record the steps.
4. Clarifying questions.

## Analyst Questions & Prompts

**[Variant A]**

Why did you perform that step? What triggers this exception? What information did you look up just now?

**[Variant B]**

"Why did you perform that manual workaround step?", "What tool or cheat-sheet did you just reference?", "How frequently does this exception occur?"

**[Variant C]**

Why did you perform that manual workaround? What information on that paper slip was critical for your decision?

**[Variant D]**

Why did you perform that manual workaround just now? What information on that physical paper strip are you checking before pressing the button?

**[Variant E]**

Can I try doing that step while you coach me? What are you looking at right now on that screen? Why did you perform that workaround just now?

**[Variant F]**

"Why do you perform that manual step?", "What error occurs most frequently during this routine?"

**[Variant G]**

'Why is this step done exactly this way?', 'What difficulties arise when something fails?'

## Minimal Realistic Example

**[Variant A]**

A BA sits with a call center agent for half a day, noticing that agents manually copy data into an unrecorded Excel spreadsheet to bypass a system bug.

**[Variant B]**

A BA passively shadows a warehouse worker for 2 hours, discovering that workers manually re-key order numbers into an Excel sheet because system auto-sync fails.

**[Variant C]**

Observing air traffic controllers handling physical paper flight strips to uncover an implicit mental model of flight progress before automating the strip display.

**[Variant D]**

Observing air traffic controllers using paper flight strips to realize they physically rearrange strips to organize mental traffic flows, yielding a requirement for flexible UI strip-reordering.

**[Variant E]**

A BA shadows an air traffic controller or financial trader in a high-stress environment, observing rapid button presses, visual eye movements, and verbal shorthand that the operator could never describe in a sit-down interview.

**[Variant F]**

An analyst watches a delivery driver interact with a vehicle terminal, noticing the driver manually re-keys addresses because screen transition is too slow.

**[Variant G]**

Observing a call-centre operator handling an incident

## Quality Criteria & Verification

**[Variant A]**

Observation conducted over a long enough period to mitigate the Hawthorne/Heisenberg effect.

**[Variant B]**

Findings validated by the observed participant; objective evidence recorded; workarounds and unwritten steps captured.

**[Variant C]**

Validation of debriefing reports with observed workers; observation across varied workload conditions (peak vs off-peak).

**[Variant D]**

Debriefing and validation of field notes with observed subjects; cross-observation under varying workload conditions.

**[Variant E]**

Practitioner review of observed workflows, identification of tacit requirements missed in official rulebooks.

**[Variant F]**

Identification of tacit workflow steps; validation of observed findings with the user.

**[Variant G]**

Comparing notes with reality, review with the employee

## Errors & Anti-Patterns

**[Variant A]**

Observing for too short a period; interrupting the worker constantly; failing to inform staff in advance; relying solely on structured checklists and missing unexpected events.

**[Variant B]**

Causing the 'Hawthorne Effect' where worker alters normal behavior because they feel evaluated; interrupting passive observation excessively.

**[Variant C]**

Hawthorne effect (people changing behavior when observed); misinterpreting observed actions; getting lost in massive irrelevant details; high cost/time overheads.

**[Variant D]**

Hawthorne effect (subjects changing behavior because they are observed); observer misinterpreting actions without SME debriefing; excessive cost/time spent collecting irrelevant detail.

**[Variant E]**

Assuming the official process rulebook reflects actual practice; interfering silently during critical operational tasks; generalizing a single user's unique habits as a universal requirement.

**[Variant F]**

Documenting the 'as-is' suboptimal process instead of designing the improved 'to-be' process.

**[Variant G]**

Hawthorne effect (people change their behaviour because they are observed)

## When NOT to Use

**[Variant A]**

When studying highly cognitive, invisible mental work without verbalization, or when statistical quantitative task-time data is required.

**[Variant B]**

For evaluating unobservable knowledge-based or cognitive-only thinking activities (e.g., high-level strategic decision making).

**[Variant C]**

When quick results are required under tight deadlines, or for brand new futuristic systems where no current operational task exists.

**[Variant D]**

When defining future, non-existent software features where no existing physical/human process exists to be observed.

**[Variant E]**

When the operational events are extremely rare (e.g., once-a-year audit tasks) or physically dangerous for an observer.

**[Variant F]**

When developing entirely new processes with no physical or operational precursor, or when procedures cannot be observed physically.

**[Variant G]**

Remote work without streaming tools, strict security restrictions
